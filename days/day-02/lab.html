Explanation lab

Part A explains a case of a model with two features in three ways at once. Part B matches questions with the methods that answer them. Part C computes Shapley values by hand for a model with three features. Parts D, E and F repeat three calculations of the lecture step by step, with sliders for their numbers: a local surrogate in the manner of LIME, local interpretable model-agnostic explanations, on one feature, permutation importance on four tumours, and the individual conditional expectation (ICE) curves, partial dependence and accumulated local effects of a product of two features. In these parts every intermediate value is rounded, and the next step continues with the rounded value, as in a calculation by hand.

Part A: Three explanations of one case

The heat map shows a model of two features, a and b, with an interaction: The effect of one feature depends on the value of the other. Click a point on the map to choose the case to explain. The panel then explains it in three ways: Exact Shapley values against the data mean, a local linear surrogate in the manner of LIME (local interpretable model-agnostic explanations) whose kernel width and seed you set, and the nearest counterfactual, drawn as a path to the decision boundary, the line on which the model output is 0.5.

The two Shapley values add up to the model output minus 0.5, the output at the data mean. A bar named LIME shows the slope of the surrogate times the distance of the case from the data mean, so that it can be compared with the Shapley value next to it. The LIME fidelity is the share of the variation of the model near the case that the surrogate reproduces, a number between 0 and 1. The formulas are on the lecture page, under the headings on LIME, on Shapley values and on choosing a method.

Part D: LIME on one feature, step by step

The worked example of the lecture: a case with the feature value \(x = 2\), counted in standard deviations, and four samples at 1, 2, 3 and 6 with the answers 0.2, 0.4, 0.6 and 0.1 of the black box. The sliders set the kernel width \(h\) and the answer of the far sample at 6. The steps compute the weights, the weighted means, the slope of the surrogate and its fidelity \(R^2\). In the figure, the size of a dot shows the weight of its sample, the grey curve the kernel and the line the surrogate.

Continue in Colab, section 4: LIME with 5000 samples on all 30 measurements of the patient.

Part E: Permutation importance by hand

The worked example of the lecture: a rule calls a tumour benign when its worst area is below a threshold. Four test tumours, benign, malignant, benign and malignant, are decided first with their own areas and then after three shuffles of the areas. The first shuffle swaps the first two areas, the second exchanges the first and the second pair, and the third reverses the order. The sliders set the threshold and the four areas. The table in the figure marks every correct decision in green and every wrong one in red.

Continue in Colab, section 2: ten shuffles of each of the 30 measurements of the forest.

Part F: ICE curves, partial dependence and accumulated local effects

The worked example of the lecture: a model that multiplies its two features, \(f(x_1, x_2) = x_1 x_2\), and three cases whose first features are 1, 2 and 3. The sliders set the second feature of each case. The steps compute the ICE curves and the partial dependence of feature 1 at \(v = 1, 2, 3\), then the local effects of the two intervals, their accumulated curve and its centring. The figure draws the three ICE curves, their average and the curve of the accumulated local effects.

Continue in Colab, section 3: ICE curves and partial dependence of the forest for any measurement.

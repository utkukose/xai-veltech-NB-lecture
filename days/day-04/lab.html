Reliability lab

Part A repeats the calibration error of ten cases step by step, with sliders for its numbers. Part B classifies four reliability diagrams by eye. Parts C and D repeat two further calculations of the lecture step by step: one prediction with two explanations, and two fairness measures. Part E computes two fairness measures by hand. Part F runs the deletion and insertion tests in the browser and measures the area under each curve, abbreviated AUC. In Parts A, C and D every intermediate value is rounded, and the next step continues with the rounded value, as in a calculation by hand.

Part A: The calibration error of ten cases, step by step

The worked example of the lecture: ten cases with their probabilities and labels, the same cases as in Python step 4. The probabilities are 0.05, 0.12, 0.35, 0.41, 0.62, 0.68, 0.81, 0.93, 0.97 and 0.22, and the labels 0, 0, 1, 0, 1, 1, 1, 1, 1 and 0. The controls choose the number of bins and whether every probability is squared, the change that keeps the order and worsens the calibration. The steps sort the cases into bins and compute the calibration error and the Brier score. The figure is the reliability diagram: one point per bin, sized by the number of its cases.

Continue in Colab, section 1: the calibration error of three credit models on 1200 test applicants.

Part B: Calibration by eye

Each diagram plots the observed frequency against the mean predicted probability in bins, for one model. The dashed diagonal is perfect calibration. Decide what each model does and press Check the diagrams.

Continue in Colab, section 1: reliability diagrams of three real models, before and after recalibration.

Part C: One prediction, two explanations

The worked example of the lecture: the model \(f(x_1, x_2)\)​\({} = \sigma(3(\sin(2 x_1) + x_2))\) gives the prediction 0.7685 at every point of the curve \(x_2 = 0.4 - \sin(2 x_1)\). The sliders place two points on this curve by their first coordinate. The steps compute the second coordinates, the prediction, the gradients, the explanations by gradient times input and their cosine similarity. The left figure shows the curve with the two points, and the right one draws the two explanations as arrows.

Continue in Colab, section 4: an attack that changes the explanation of a real tumour.

Part D: Two fairness measures, step by step

The worked example of the lecture: a model decides about 200 applicants of group A, of whom 100 would repay, and 150 of group B, of whom 60 would repay. The sliders set how many applicants of each kind the model approves. The steps compute the approval rate, the true positive rate and the false positive rate of both groups and the two differences of the lecture. The figure compares the three rates of the two groups.

Continue in Colab, section 5: the fairness audit of the credit model and its proxy.

Part F: Deletion and insertion

A model of eight features explains twenty cases. Choose an explanation method and run the deletion and insertion tests: Features that support the prediction are removed first, or added first, and the probability of the predicted class is recorded. A faithful explanation makes the deletion curve fall fast and the insertion curve rise fast. Every run is compared with a random ranking, which any method must beat.

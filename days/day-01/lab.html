Glass-box lab

Part A compares interpretable models on simulated tumours with two measurements and records accuracy against the number of values a reader must hold in mind. Part B places eight explanations from practice on the map of methods. Part C follows five patients through a small decision tree by hand. Parts D, E and F repeat three calculations of the lecture step by step, with sliders for their numbers: one prediction of a logistic regression, the first question of a tree and the area under the curve (AUC) by counting pairs. In these parts every intermediate value is rounded, and the next step continues with the rounded value, as in a calculation by hand.

Part A: Accuracy against readability

Choose a model family and its setting, then press Run and add to scoreboard. The panel shows the decision regions on held-out tumours. Every run is added to the scoreboard below as one point: Test accuracy against the number of values a reader must hold in mind to reproduce a prediction.

Scoreboard

Find the run with the highest accuracy for the fewest values. Then increase the depth until the accuracy stops improving: The point where the frontier flattens is the place to stop adding complexity.

Part D: One prediction of a logistic regression, step by step

The worked example of the lecture: a logistic regression with three features, the weights \(-0.9\), \(-1.2\) and \(0.7\) and the bias \(b = 0.3\). The sliders set the scaled values of one patient, each counted in standard deviations from the mean of the training tumours, and the bias. The steps compute the contributions, the score, the probability of benign and the odds. The left figure adds the contributions up to the score, and the right one places the score on the logistic curve.

Continue in Colab, section 3: the contributions of every test patient for all 30 measurements.

Part E: The first question of a tree, step by step

The worked example of the lecture: The root of the tree holds the 426 training tumours, 159 malignant and 267 benign. A question sends some of them to the left and the rest to the right. The sliders set how many malignant and how many benign tumours go to the left. The steps compute the Gini impurity of the root, of both sides and of the split, and the share of training tumours that the question alone classifies correctly. The left figure shows the classes at the root and on both sides, and the right one places the three nodes on the curve \(G = 2p(1 - p)\).

Continue in Colab, section 4: a tree of depth 3 grown by the same rule on all 30 measurements.

Part F: An AUC by counting pairs

The worked example of the lecture: three benign and two malignant tumours with their probabilities of benign. Every pair of one benign and one malignant tumour is checked. It counts as correctly ordered when the benign tumour has the higher probability, and as one half when both probabilities are equal. The sliders set the five probabilities, and the figure places the tumours on the axis of probabilities and draws every pair that has been checked.

Continue in Colab, section 5: seven models compared on all 143 test tumours.
